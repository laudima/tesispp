\begin{abstract}
Observational data alone does not, in general, identify a unique causal structure, so causal discovery relies on interventions, distributional assumptions, or background knowledge. Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as a source of such knowledge, proposing edges, priors, or whole graphs from variable names. This practice presupposes that their causal judgments track causal structure rather than the probabilistic association of the words that describe the variables. Drawing on Pearl's ladder of causation and Rosen's theory of anticipatory systems, as developed by Astorga and Letelier, we predicted that a system that compresses text statistics would let words override the structure it is given. We tested this on CLadder questions that state the same causal graph and probabilities with plausible, implausible, or invented variable names. In four light open-weight models, the names carried a strong prior when a question was asked on its own, but the given context largely removed it, contrary to our prediction. The context was not, however, used as structure: accuracy stayed close to chance, and the smaller models answered from the wording of the question rather than from the graph. We discuss what this implies for LLMs in causal discovery.

\keywords{Causal discovery \and Large language models \and Causal reasoning \and Pearl's causal hierarchy \and Anticipatory systems}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Observational data cannot by itself reproduce a unique causal structure. Distinct directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) can be Markov equivalent and therefore indistinguishable from the distribution they generate \cite{verma1990equivalence,spirtes2000causation,glymour2019review}. Some causal discovery methods try to reduce this ambiguity by using interventional data, assuming properties of the data distribution (e.g., LiNGAM \cite{shimizu2006linear} and FASK \cite{sanchez2019estimating}, which use non-Gaussianity), or by adding background knowledge that creates or forbids particular edges \cite{spirtes2016causal}. Background knowledge has come from domain experts, human knowledge, experiments, or simulations. Recently, LLMs have been proposed as a possible substitute or source. They have been queried for pairwise orientations, edge priors, or full graphs from variable names and textual metadata, and their answers are passed to discovery or estimation pipelines \cite{long2023can,kiciman2023causal,zhang2023understanding,verma2025causal}.

The use of LLMs takes as a point of departure that they can create informative and genuine causal relationships and 
understand the causal structure behind the data. Different perspectives are listed;  Kıcıman et al.\ 
mention high accuracy in pairwise orientation and counterfactual tasks \cite{kiciman2023causal}, while others argue that such success reflects causal facts that are stated in the training text and recited back, rather than causal inference \cite{willig2022can, Zecevic2023CausalPL}, and that performance degrades when the context is limited \cite{hobbhahn2022investigating,li2023counterfactual,chen2024clear}.

We use light open-weight LLMs to answer causal questions using the query's structure and context, a step-by-step reasoning prompt, and a direct question. We use CLadder \cite{jin2023cladder}, which states the causal graph and the required probabilities in each prompt and realizes the same formal problem with \textbf{\textcolor{cComm}{commonsensical}} (``smoking causes cancer''), \textbf{\textcolor{cAnti}{anti-commonsensical}} (``smoking affects ear shape''), and \textbf{\textcolor{cNons}{nonsensical}} (``zory affects qixy'') variable names. If the given structure determines the answer, the three variants should be equally hard. If word association dominates, anti-commonsensical items should be harder, and the accuracy should drop.

Our contributions are: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item A theoretical background, combining Pearl's ladder of causation \cite{pearl2009causality,pearl2018theoretical} with Rosen's theory of anticipatory systems \cite{rosen2012anticipatory} as developed by Astorga and Letelier \cite{astorga2026anticipation}, of why an LLM should be expected to let tokens override given structure
    \item A falsifiable prediction, tested on matched questions that differ only in the variable names, recording each model's probability of answering ``Yes'' rather than only its final answer. 
    \item Analysis of the results in the four light open-weight models: the variable names carry a strong plausibility prior that changes the accuracy compared with when the question is asked alone, showcasing that the given context overrides this prior, with or without step-by-step reasoning, and the causal structure is not retrieved by any of the models
    \item Discussion of what this implies for LLMs in causal discovery
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Breaking Markov equivalence}
\label{sec:mec}

Traditional methods, like the PC algorithm and Fast Causal Inference (FCI) \cite{spirtes2000causation}, recover the Markov equivalence class from conditional independence tests, relying on the causal Markov and faithfulness conditions \cite{glymour2019review}. The class can contain many DAGs that disagree on edge orientation, and the observational distribution cannot decide between them. Four families of additional information can help decide: \textit{Interventions} change the distribution in a way that depends on edge direction. \emph{Temporal order} rules out effects that precede their causes. \emph{Distributional assumptions} make the full DAG identifiable. LiNGAM shows that when the relations are linear, there are no unobserved confounders, and the disturbances are non-Gaussian, independent component analysis recovers the complete causal order without any prior ordering of the variables \cite{shimizu2006linear}; FASK combines a fast adjacency search with orientation rules based on non-Gaussian signals and can recover feedback, including two-cycles, in fMRI time series \cite{sanchez2019estimating}. \emph{Background knowledge} fixes or forbids particular edges \cite{spirtes2016causal,peters2017elements}.

The first three families (\emph{interventions, temporal order, distributional assumptions}) rest on explicit, testable assumptions about how the data were produced. Background knowledge serves as a ground truth for the data's reliability. When the source is an LLM, the question is what that reliability is grounded in, compared to usual physical or simulated experiments.

\subsection{Can LLMs serve for background knowledge?}
\label{sec:llm-bk}

Long et al.\ used LLMs to score candidate edges in medical causal graphs and found the scores useful but dependent on the choice of words, context, and prompt \cite{long2023can}. Kıcıman et al.\ reported that GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 reach 97\% accuracy on a pairwise causal discovery benchmark, operating only on variable names and metadata, and noted unpredictable failure modes \cite{kiciman2023causal}. Zhang et al.\ argued that current LLMs can answer causal questions that draw on existing domain knowledge but not those that require discovering new knowledge \cite{zhang2023understanding}. Verma et al.\ proposed an LLM agent that selects statistical methods for applied causal inference \cite{verma2025causal}. In these pipelines, the LLM takes the role of the domain expert; its outputs are rarely validated against interventional evidence.

\subsection{Evaluating causal reasoning in LLMs}
\label{sec:eval}

 Willig et al.\ and Zečević et al.\ introduce the \textit{meta structural causal model}; the variables encode causal facts about other models. When LLMs succeed at causal questions, they are reproducing correlations between such facts in their training text; they call such systems ``causal parrots'' \cite{willig2022can,Zecevic2023CausalPL}. Hobbhahn et al.\ keep the same meaning of a prompt fixed while changing its presentation and find that LLMs perform worse when form and content differ \cite{hobbhahn2022investigating}. Li et al.\ use counterfactual premises that contradict world knowledge (``if cats were vegetarians'' - anti-commonsensical premises) to separate the use of the hypothetical premise from the use of real-world knowledge and associative cues \cite{li2023counterfactual}. Chen et al.\ test whether models understand causal graphs themselves across graph-based tasks and find only a preliminary understanding in the models \cite{chen2024clear}.

We used the CLadder \cite{jin2023cladder} dataset of causal queries; each question is generated from an explicit graph and query type, and the causal inference is included with the correct answer. Jin et al.\ built the anti-commonsensical and nonsensical variants to control for memorization. In their main results, GPT-4 was 1.8 points less accurate on anti-commonsensical than on commonsensical items, and their CausalCoT prompting raised anti-commonsensical accuracy by 9.65 points; the smaller open models they tested, LLaMA and Alpaca (6.7B), were near chance overall \cite{jin2023cladder}. Their experiments give no information about current light open-weight models or about the probabilities behind the answers. A contrasting result is that of Butkus and Kriegeskorte, who show that a transformer trained by next-token prediction on data generated from linear Gaussian structural causal models, including interventional data, learns to answer counterfactual queries and carries an internal model trained to answer on a synthetic language \cite{butkus2025causal}. We return to this result in Sect.~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Theoretical Framework}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Seeing, doing, and imagining}
\label{sec:ladder}

Pearl organizes causal reasoning into three rungs, which correspond to three cognitive abilities: seeing, doing, and imagining \cite{pearl2009causality,pearl2018bookOfWhy}. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Association} (L1) concerns regularities in observation, $P(y \mid x)$: what does a symptom tell me about a disease? 
    \item \textit{Intervention} (L2) concerns the effect of setting a variable, $P(y \mid do(x))$: what happens to $Y$ if I make $X$ happen? 
    \item \textit{Counterfactuals} (L3) concern a specific unit under an alternative that did not occur, $P(y_x \mid x', y')$: would my migraine have gone away had I taken an aspirin?
\end{itemize}
 Pearl argues that systems fitted to large collections of observations, like a statistician fitting a curve to a set of points, remain on the first rung, and therefore cannot adapt to circumstances outside their data. \cite{pearl2018theoretical,pearl2018bookOfWhy}. Pearl's argument, however, concerns systems whose data are observations of the variables in question. The training data of an LLM are not observations; they are text, and the text may contain descriptions of interventions and statements of causal facts \cite{Zecevic2023CausalPL}. Although association may be present, this does not settle whether an LLM can answer L2 and L3 questions. What it needs in addition is an account of what distinguishes a system that has a causal model of something from a system that summarizes correlations in what others have written about it. 

\subsection{Reactive and anticipatory systems}
\label{sec:rosen}

Rosen distinguishes \emph{reactive} systems, whose future states are computed from present and past states, from \emph{anticipatory} systems, which contain a predictive model of themselves and of their environment \cite{rosen2012anticipatory}. Modeling is done via the encoding of a natural system($NS$); the system creates a causal entailment that corresponds to the natural structure and uses inferential entailment in a mathematical formal system to predict the natural system. A formal system that reproduces the observables of a natural system without this correspondence is not a model, whatever its predictive accuracy (it is a simulation).

Astorga and Letelier connect Rosen's anticipatory systems to Maturana's structural coupling and describe three levels at which an observer can describe an organism \cite{astorga2026anticipation}. At Level~0, the organism associates signals with actions and the objects that produce the signals are assumed to exist beforehand. At Level~1, these associations are described by a conditional probability matrix $P(Y \mid s_\alpha)$ over signals and actions, which they characterize as ``the observer's statistical description of the effective behavior'' of the organism. At Level~2, the objects are no longer given; they are built and maintained through the organism's recurrent $<perception, action>$ interaction with its medium, and constitute its \emph{Umwelt}. Level~1 is the analog of Pearl's first rung: a table of conditional regularities with no account of where the regularities come from.

On this basis, Astorga and Letelier classify current AI systems, including LLMs, as reactive. They ``contain statistical compressions of training data'', a conditional distribution over next tokens is ``a summary of past correlations, not a dynamical system with its own internal entailment running ahead of reality'', and an LLM's Umwelt ``is not self-constructed but derived from others' Umwelten'' \cite{astorga2026anticipation}. They separate two claims. The \textit{architectural} claim is that current models lack internal dynamics coupled to the environment. The \textit{constitutive} claim is that anticipation requires self-fabrication, so no purely computational system can meet it. Other perspectives think that computational enactivism offers a contrasting view: by using the free energy principle, cognitive systems can encode generative models of their niches and update them through both $<perception,action>$, and this process is at once computational and enactive \cite{korbak2021computational}.  We take Astorga and Letelier's position as our theoretical frame; experimental results can only support the architectural claim, showing that a model's answers follow the statistics of its training text rather than the structure it is given. 

The Markov and faithfulness conditions can hold for the system described in a CLadder question; however, the model's output distribution is not a distribution over that system's variables. It is a distribution over text, produced by people describing their own \textit{Umwelten}. Dependencies in that distribution reflect how causal relations are talked about, which is the meta-model of Zečević et al.\ \cite{Zecevic2023CausalPL}, and there is no Markov or faithfulness link between them and the structure given in the prompt. Pearl's hierarchy tells us that L2 and L3 answers require structure; the Markov and faithfulness conditions tell us when statistics can reveal structure; Rosen's modeling relation, in Astorga and Letelier's reading, tells us why a statistical compression of text does not function as an architecture for the modeling of a natural system. 

\subsection{Hypothesis}
\label{sec:predictions}

If a model answers based on the structure in the prompt, the words used to name the variables should not matter. If it answers from a statistical compression of text, those words should influence the answer even when the given graph and probabilities determine it. Nonsensical names carry no semantic relation. Anti-commonsensical names, such as an effect of ``playing jazz'' on ``cancer diagnosis'', carry a prior against the stated relation, and should affect the result negatively. We therefore present some hypotheses for the experimental results. 

\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{H1}] Accuracy on nonsensical items is close to accuracy on commonsensical items, and accuracy on anti-commonsensical items is lower than both.
\item[\textbf{H1b}] Accuracy on commonsensical queries is better than anti-commonsensical and  nonsensical
\item[\textbf{H2}] Accuracy on L2 and L3 questions is lower than on L1 questions. 
\end{itemize}

Low accuracy across all variants would not support the account, since it is equally consistent with a model that simply cannot perform the computation.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}



\subsection{CLadder Data Set}
\label{sec:sampling}

CLadder \cite{jin2023cladder} contains yes/no questions with a causal graph, a query type, and a story. Each item consists of a question $q_i$, a binary answer $a_i \in \{\text{Yes},\text{No}\}$, and an explanation $e_i$. 

The commonsensical and anti-commonsensical files of CLadder v1 contain the same formal questions with one variable renamed; the same story, graph, query type, given probabilities, and answer are shared. We matched them one-to-one on these fields, obtaining 9,758 unambiguous pairs; for example, ``Will smoking increase the chance of lung cancer?'' is paired with ``Will listening to jazz increase the chance of lung cancer?'' under identical probabilities. Nonsensical items use different parameters and are compared as a group. We drew 1,000 pairs (commonsensical and anti-commonsensical) and 1,000 nonsensical items, giving 3,000 items that every model answers. Comparisons between commonsensical and anti-commonsensical items are therefore paired: any difference is attributable to the names alone.

\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}

We tested four smaller AI models: SmolLM2-1.7B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, Phi-3.5-mini-instruct (3.8B), and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. We ran SmolLM2 and Qwen2.5-1.5B using full precision (32-bit) because they fit on the GPU, which made their 'Yes' probabilities consistent. Phi-3.5 and Qwen2.5-7B were too large, so we used a special method (4-bit quantization and 16-bit computation) to load them. This means their 'Yes' probabilities might vary slightly depending on how items are grouped. All models answered the same items in each run, grouped in batches of 8 for the direct and question-only runs and 32 for the CausalCoT run. We recorded all results with details about each model's settings.

\subsection{Three runs}
\label{sec:runs}

Every run asks the model for a single Yes/No answer and reads, instead of generating and parsing text, a  probability is assign to each answer:
\[
P(\text{Yes}) = \frac{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{Y}} e^{\ell_t}}{\sum_{t \in \mathcal{Y}} e^{\ell_t} + \sum_{t \in \mathcal{N}} e^{\ell_t}},
\]
where $\ell_t$ is the logit of token $t$ and $\mathcal{Y}$, $\mathcal{N}$ are the single-token forms of ``Yes'' and ``No''. The answer is ``Yes'' when $P(\text{Yes}) > 0.5$, and $P(\text{Yes})$ is kept as a graded measure of the model's preference. We conducted three runs, each with a different prompt. The direct and CausalCoT runs use the three types of questions (commonsensical, anti-commonsensical, nonsensical); the question-only run uses the matched commonsensical and anti-commonsensical pairs.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Direct.} The model receives the CLadder background, which states the causal graph, the given probabilities, the question, and ``Answer with Yes or No only.'' This run tests whether the answer follows the given structure and whether it changes with the type of question. We ran it with 1,000 commonsensical, 1,000 anti-commonsensical, and 1,000 nonsensical items per model.
  \\
  \item \emph{CausalCoT.} The model first writes out its reasoning following the six steps of Jin et al.\ \cite{jin2023cladder} (extract the graph, identify the query type, formalize it, gather the data, derive the estimand, compute it), and is then asked for the Yes/No answer. This run tests whether step-by-step reasoning helps small models use the structure. It was added after the direct run and uses a subset of 150 pairs and 150 nonsensical items per model.
  \\
  \item \emph{Question-only.} The model receives only the question and ``Answer with Yes or No only.'', without the variables, graph or the probabilities. Its $P(\text{Yes})$ is then the model's \emph{prior} for the question as worded, which is what the variable names alone suggest. This run checks the assumption behind H1 and H1b ( that anti-commonsensical names carry a prior against the stated relation).
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

We report accuracy for each model and run, separately for the three types of question. Every comparison is a difference between two groups, reported with its 95\% confidence interval and tested with one of two tests (two-sided, $\alpha = 0.05$):
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textit{paired t-test} between a commonsensical question and its anti-commonsensical twin 
\item A \emph{two-proportion z-test} comparing commonsensical versus nonsensical questions.
\end{itemize}
Each null hypothesis ($H_0$) states that the change being compared, such as renaming a variable or adding the graph to the prompt, has no effect on the model's answers. Table~\ref{tab:summary} lists them together with the results.

The CausalCoT run is exploratory, and we report it descriptively.  And only differences with the 95\% interval for the sample. 


For the question-only run, we measure the mean difference in $P(\text{Yes})$ between the commonsensical and the anti-commonsensical question of each pair, asked alone. We test it against the same gap when the question is asked in context (direct run), using paired t-tests. The key comparison is whether adding the graph and the probabilities shrinks the gap, which we test with a paired t-test on the per-pair difference between the two gaps. To separate the prior from the use of the structure, we compare for each model the in-context $\operatorname{logit} P(\text{Yes})$ with the question-only $\operatorname{logit} P(\text{Yes})$ on the correct answer, and report the share of variance. The study is exploratory and was designed to test a sample of the full dataset to evaluate our three strategies and the different behavior across the four models.

\subsection{Direct run}
\label{sec:res-direct}

All four models answer almost by chance. The accuracy is between 0.50 and 0.60 for every model and type of question (Table~\ref{tab:variants}, Fig.~\ref{fig:acc-variant}). Table~\ref{tab:differences} lists the differences. The type of question makes no difference. For every model, the difference between commonsensical and anti-commonsensical questions, and between commonsensical and nonsensical ones, is below 0.04 and not significant (all $p \geq 0.10$), so H1 is not supported, and neither are H1b and H2. Higher rungs did not present an accuracy drop; where the rungs differ, L2 or L3 is \emph{more} accurate than L1, the opposite of the prediction. The largest difference is for Qwen2.5-7B, whose accuracy rises from 0.52 at L1 to 0.64 at L2 ($p < 0.001$).

The models are also strongly biased. SmolLM2 answers ``No'' to all but 0.1\% of the items, so its accuracy simply reflects the share of ``No'' answers in each group. Other models give a fixed answer to some query types: Phi-3.5 and Qwen2.5-7B answer ``No'' to every collider-bias question, whatever the graph and the numbers.

\subsection{CausalCoT run}
\label{sec:res-cot}

Reasoning step-by-step did not improve the models (Table~\ref{tab:cot-variants}, Fig.~\ref{fig:acc-cot}). On the same 450 items, accuracy changes by $-0.007$ for SmolLM2, $+0.047$ for Qwen2.5-1.5B, and $+0.022$ for Phi-3.5, all within sampling variation ($\pm 0.07$). Only Qwen2.5-7B improves, from 0.57 to 0.64 ($+0.073$), slightly more than the sampling variation. For the two smallest models, reasoning mainly shifts the answers toward ``Yes'' rather than toward the correct answer. The types of questions did not differ: no model's commonsensical accuracy differs clearly from its nonsensical or its anti-commonsensical accuracy after reasoning (with 150 items per type, every difference is within sampling variation, $\pm 0.11$). The reasoning traces suggest why reasoning helps so little: deterministic counterfactual questions give no probabilities, yet 81\% of Qwen2.5-1.5B and 74\% of SmolLM2 traces on these items state probability values, against 12\% for Phi-3.5 and 9\% for Qwen2.5-7B (43 items per model).

\subsection{Question-only run}
\label{sec:res-prior}

The only large and consistent differences between types of question appear when the question is asked alone. Without the graph and the probabilities, every model assigns much less probability to ``Yes'' on the anti-commonsensical question than on its commonsensical twin (Table~\ref{tab:prior}). The prior gap is between $0.09$ (SmolLM2) and $0.23$ (Qwen2.5-7B), with every interval far from zero, and the anti-commonsensical question receives the lower $P(\text{Yes})$ in 73--78\% of pairs. The names therefore carry the prior that H1 and H1b assume. When the same questions are asked with the graph and the probabilities, the gap shrinks by 83--96\% for every model ($p < 0.001$), to between $-0.023$ and $+0.037$. Implying their answers follow how the question is worded.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Probability of words dominates.} By the criterion stated in Sect.~\ref{sec:predictions}, the absence of an accuracy gap and of a consistent shift in $P(\text{Yes})$ between commonsensical and anti-commonsensical items counts against the word-plausibility account for these models, with or without step-by-step reasoning. This agrees with Jin et al., who found only a 1.8-point gap for GPT-4 \cite{jin2023cladder}. The question-only run shows that the models are influenced by the probability of the token on which the questions are worded. In general, the anti-commonsensical question receives 0.09--0.23 less probability of ``Yes''. With the graph and the numbers in the prompt, this gap shrinks by 83--96\%, to at most 0.04. 

\paragraph{Limits on open-weight models.} Overall accuracy in this size of models are near chance, several query types receive the same answer whatever question, the reasoning traces often invent data, and for the two smallest models the in-context answer follows the question-only prior and not the correct answer. We can relate the results to the reactive-systems framework of Sect.~\ref{sec:rosen}, in which the answer responds to the form of the input rather than to give an output of the structure. Reasoning helped only Qwen2.5-7B, which is the larger model; other reasoning models with even higher parameters could 
improve our current results considerably. Butkus and Kriegeskorte's result \cite{butkus2025causal}, where next-token prediction on data generated by causal models yields causal inference, is consistent with the architectural problem these LLMs present: these models may lack training on the causal structure and syntactic form of the query. 



\paragraph{LLM on causal discovery.} We can related the current two runs for causal discovery. First, when asked about a relation between named variables and nothing else, as in pairwise edge queries \cite{kiciman2023causal,long2023can}, the models will answer according to the probability of the variable names in the relation. Given explicit structure and data, they reduce the prior but do not answer causal inferences with high accuracy; the answer is never grounded in the causal structure of the system. Causal relationships used in discovery should therefore be grounded in experiments, simulations, or explicit distributional assumptions such as those of LiNGAM and FASK. 

\paragraph{Limitations.} We tested four small models that are not trained as reasoning models. We used a single prompt wording per condition; distinct prompts or other strategies can change the overall results . CLadder gives the graph and the information in the prompt, so it tests inference from a given structure, which differs from obtaining a structure given data. The CausalCoT prompt was added for exploratory analysis; it uses only 150 items per type and model (differences within $\pm 0.11$ cannot be distinguished from sampling variation), and 3--8\% of its traces (model output) were truncated.
The question-only prompt is unusual for the models, which mostly answer ``No'' to it (3--16\% ``Yes''), so the prior is measured by the probability of ``Yes'' rather than by the answers. Finally, thresholding $P(\text{Yes})$ at 0.5 mixes answer bias into accuracy; we could measure yes or no answers by parsing. 

\paragraph{Practical challenges.} A first run of SmolLM2 on all 10,560 items of the CLadder v1 easy file, generating and parsing answers, took 31 hours and lost answers to over-length and unreadable responses; it was near chance (0.47) with 64\% ``yes'' answers. For the CausalCoT condition, reasoning traces of 1024 tokens were often too short for all queries. The cost of evaluating higher models on a bigger sample is still a major limitation. 


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

LLMs are being increasingly used as domain experts in causal discovery. Drawing on Pearl's ladder of causation and Rosen's distinction between reactive and anticipatory systems, we argued that a system that compresses text statistics would let the probability of variable names override the causal structure it is given. On matched CLadder questions that differ only in the names, four light open-weight models showed no significant effect on accuracy, and the exploratory step-by-step reasoning run did not change this. The questions alone revealed what Astorga and Letelier concluded. When we added the graph and probabilities, this prior's effect shrank to at most 0.04, but the models still did not use the structure, and reasoning improved accuracy only for the largest model, and not by much. We suggest discarding LLMs for causal structure discovery. As shown in the results, even with larger models, there is a high chance that the probability of the tokens exceeds the probability of the data itself. Putting a bias that makes it incompatible with what it means to have a causal relationship under other types of experiments.
